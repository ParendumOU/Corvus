48 hours in Bergen, Europe's rainiest city

It rains here on more than 200 days a year. Locals barely notice, and after a weekend of fish markets, funiculars and fjords, neither will you.

By Ingrid Solberg · 3 min read · Updated 18 October

Bergen has a reputation, and it has earned it. Wedged between seven mountains on Norway's west coast, the city catches every weather front rolling in off the North Sea. But the rain is part of the charm: it keeps the hills green, the harbour moody and the cafés full.

The trick is to stop waiting for a dry window and simply go. Bergen is compact enough to cross on foot in half an hour, every museum and market has a warm room to duck into, and the light after a shower, when the clouds tear open over the fjord, is the best you will see all week.

Day one: Bryggen and the fish market

Start at Bryggen, the row of UNESCO-listed Hanseatic wharf houses that leans over the harbour. Wander the narrow wooden passages behind the facades before the tour groups arrive, then walk five minutes to the fish market for shrimp sandwiches and smoked salmon.

In the afternoon, cross to the Hanseatic Museum to see how German merchants lived and traded here for four centuries: cramped sleeping cabinets, heavy ledgers and a winter assembly room that was the only heated space on the wharf. Then follow the waterfront to Nordnes park, where locals swim in the sea pool even in drizzle.

For dinner, book a table in one of the old warehouses along the quay. Order the catch of the day and a bowl of fish soup, the city's unofficial national dish, and stay for the lights coming on across the water.

If you still have energy, walk up to the Skansen fire station at dusk. The streets around it are the oldest in the city, the gas lamps come on one by one, and you will have the view almost to yourself.

Up Mount Fløyen

The Fløibanen funicular climbs 320 metres in eight minutes. At the top, the whole city spreads out below you. Even on grey days the view is worth it, and the forest trails behind the station are quiet within minutes.

If the weather holds, skip the ride down and walk instead. The forest path drops back into town through steep lanes of white wooden houses, past tiny gardens and staircases that seem to belong to another century. It takes about forty minutes and ends a short stroll from the cathedral.

Early risers should go up before nine. The first cars are almost empty, the mist is still lifting off the harbour and the café at the top serves cinnamon buns warm from the oven.

Day two: into the fjords

Book the Mostraumen cruise, a three-hour round trip from the harbour through narrow straits and past waterfalls you can almost touch. Sit outside for the best views, and bring a layer: the wind on the water is cold even in July.

The boat leaves from the fish market quay and slips out past the islands into Osterfjord, where the mountains rise straight out of the water and the villages are reachable only by sea. Halfway, it squeezes through Mostraumen, a channel so narrow you could throw a stone onto either bank, and pauses under a waterfall long enough for everyone on deck to get soaked. Nobody minds. Book a morning departure in summer; the afternoon boats fill up with cruise passengers.

Back in town, spend the evening at KODE, four art museums in a row along the city lake, with one of the largest collections of Edvard Munch outside Oslo. Most rooms are open late on Thursdays, and the museum shop is a good place to wait out a downpour.

What to pack

A compact, wind-proof umbrella is non-negotiable, along with a light waterproof shell and shoes that can handle cobbles in the rain. Locals have a saying: there is no bad weather, only bad clothing.

Bring layers rather than one heavy coat: the temperature barely moves between seasons, but it can swing several degrees between a sheltered street and the open quay. A small dry bag keeps your phone and camera safe on the boat, and a pair of wool socks will save the second day if your shoes give up on the first.

Most of all, pack a little patience. The weather here changes every hour, and the best plan is the one you are happy to rearrange. Sit by a window, order another coffee, and wait for the next break in the clouds. It always comes.
